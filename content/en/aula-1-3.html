Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  1. distinguish slots, blocks and epochs;
  2. explain at a high level how stake contributes to leader selection;
  3. formulate a confirmation policy without promising instant finality.
Before you begin

Revisit the roles of consensus and the ledger in Lesson 1.2.

Guiding question

Does every slot contain a block, and does an observed block make a transaction irreversible?

1. Slots, blocks and epochs

Slots are numbered time intervals used by the protocol. Epochs group slots and serve as a reference for parameters and activities that evolve in cycles. A slot may produce no block, and a block may contain several transactions. Thus, slots, blocks and transactions do not have a one-to-one relationship.

Times displayed by an interface are conversions performed by tools. For a reproducible observation, preserve the network, slot, block, source and query time. A human-readable date without the network context does not replace the technical identifier.

2. Stake, pools and leadership

ADA holders can delegate the participation associated with that value, called stake, to a pool. The pool operator maintains the infrastructure for producing blocks. Delegation neither transfers ownership of the ADA nor hands over the payment key: the operator cannot spend the delegator's funds.

In the Ouroboros family, the active stake associated with a pool influences its chance of producing blocks. A larger share increases that chance but does not guarantee a block in every interval. Selection follows verifiable protocol rules, without a central coordinator choosing the next producer.

A block producer proposes an extension to the chain. Other nodes still verify the block and apply consensus and ledger rules. Producing a block does not confer authority to change the rules arbitrarily.

3. Observed inclusion and operational confidence

A transaction observed in a block is included in the chain seen by that source at that moment. Competing candidate chains may exist near the tip. Applications wait for additional depth or another operational condition according to value, risk and tolerance.

The protocol provides probabilistic properties under security assumptions. The application needs to state its policy rather than simply label a transaction “final”. An interface can distinguish at least three states: submitted to the endpoint, included in the observed chain and accepted under the internal policy once the defined condition is met.

An appropriately qualified conclusion

“Included in the observed block and accepted under the configured confirmation policy” is more precise than “irreversible”. The policy should record its source, condition and version.

4. What consensus does not prove

Consensus organizes a history of valid transactions. It does not automatically verify the legal identity of whoever controls a key, the institutional purpose of a transfer or the truth of a document referenced by hash. These questions reappear in the lessons on signatures, metadata and evidence.

Check answer

Submitted: the endpoint received the packet. Included: an identified source shows the tx id in a block and slot. Accepted internally: the depth, time or corroboration condition defined for the operation has been met. The first option records the state without going beyond the evidence.

What we learned

  • Slots organize block production opportunities; epochs group slots. A slot and a block are not the same thing.
  • Stake contributes to leader selection and protocol security.
  • Observing a transaction's inclusion and accepting it operationally are different decisions. The confirmation policy must be explicit.
  • Consensus does not prove the truth of an external fact recorded in a transaction.
